Part A · Hypertension & Blood Pressure Control · Unit 01

What Is Hypertension?

Definition

Hypertension is defined as either a sustained systolic blood pressure of greater than 140 mm Hg or a sustained diastolic blood pressure of greater than 90 mm Hg.

Cause

↑ Peripheral vascular arteriolar smooth muscle tone→↑ Arteriolar resistance+↓ Capacitance of the venous system
Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Vascular Resistance

Classification

Hypertension is classified into four categories for the purpose of treatment management.

CategorySystolic (mm Hg)Diastolic (mm Hg)
Normal<120and<80
Prehypertension120–139or80–89
Stage I140–159or90–99
Stage II≥160or≥100
Read the connector

“Normal” requires both values (systolic and diastolic) to be below the limit; every other category is assigned if either value (systolic or diastolic) falls in its range.

Quick Review

  • Hypertension = sustained systolic BP >140 mm Hg or sustained diastolic BP >90 mm Hg.
  • Cause: ↑ peripheral vascular arteriolar smooth muscle tone → ↑ arteriolar resistance and ↓ capacitance of the venous system.
  • Blood Pressure = Cardiac Output × Peripheral Vascular Resistance.
  • Four categories: Normal <120 and <80 · Prehypertension 120–139 or 80–89 · Stage I 140–159 or 90–99 · Stage II ≥160 or ≥100.

Part A · Hypertension & Blood Pressure Control · Unit 02

Mechanisms for Controlling Blood Pressure

Cardiac output and peripheral resistance are controlled mainly by two mechanisms:

1 · The baroreflexes

Act through the sympathetic nervous system.

2 · The renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system

The kidney’s long-term control through blood volume.

Key principle

Most antihypertensive drugs lower blood pressure by (A) reducing cardiac output and/or (B) decreasing peripheral resistance.

A. Baroreceptors and the Sympathetic Nervous System

Baroreflexes act by changing the activity of the sympathetic nervous system.

Baroreflex response to a fall in blood pressure
Fall in blood pressure
↓
Pressure-sensitive neurons (baroreceptors) send fewer impulses to cardiovascular centers in the spinal cord
↓
Reflex response: ↑ sympathetic and ↓ parasympathetic output to the heart and vasculature
↓
Vasoconstriction + increased cardiac output
↓
Compensatory RISE in blood pressure

B. Renin-Angiotensin-Aldosterone System (RAAS)

The kidney regulates long-term blood pressure by controlling blood volume. When blood pressure or sodium levels fall, the kidney releases renin, which converts angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin II via ACE.

Renin-angiotensin system — sequence of events
  1. Trigger: drop in blood pressure / drop in fluid volume.
  2. Renin release from kidney.
  3. The liver supplies angiotensinogen; renin acts on angiotensinogen to form angiotensin I.
  4. ACE (angiotensin-converting enzyme) is released from the lungs; ACE acts on angiotensin I to form angiotensin II.
  5. Angiotensin II acts directly on blood vessels, stimulating vasoconstriction (narrowing).
  6. Angiotensin II acts on the adrenal gland to stimulate release of aldosterone.
  7. Aldosterone acts on the kidneys to stimulate reabsorption of salt (NaCl) and water (H2O).

Quick Review

  • CO and peripheral resistance are controlled mainly by the baroreflexes and the RAAS.
  • Most antihypertensives lower BP by reducing cardiac output and/or decreasing peripheral resistance.
  • Fall in BP → baroreceptors send fewer impulses → ↑ sympathetic, ↓ parasympathetic → vasoconstriction + ↑ CO → compensatory rise in BP.
  • Kidney controls long-term BP via blood volume: renin converts angiotensinogen → angiotensin I → angiotensin II (via ACE from the lungs).
  • Angiotensin II: vasoconstriction; stimulates aldosterone (↑ Na+/water reabsorption → ↑ volume); constricts the efferent arteriole.

Part B · Diuretics · Unit 05

Thiazides and Related Agents

A. Thiazides

Thiazide structure shown in the handout; the sulfonamide group (H2N–SO2–) is at the top left.

Chlorothiazide, hydrochlorothiazide, chlorthalidone, indapamide and metolazone are referred to as thiazide-like diuretics, because:

  • they contain the sulfonamide residue in their chemical structures, and
  • their mechanism of action is similar.
Thiazide diureticBrand (as listed)
ChlorothiazideDIURIL
ChlorthalidoneGeneric only
Hydrochlorothiazide (HCTZ)MICROZIDE
IndapamideGeneric only
MetolazoneZAROXOLYN

1. Mechanism of action

Main siteCortical region of the ascending loop of Henle and the distal tubule
ActionDecrease the reabsorption of Na+, apparently by inhibition of a Na+/Cl− co-transporter on the luminal membrane of the tubules
Proximal tubuleLesser effect
Result↑ Concentration of Na+ and Cl− in the tubular fluid
Acid-base balanceNot usually affected

2. Actions

a. Increased excretion of Na+ and Cl−: thiazide diuretics cause diuresis with increased Na+ and Cl− excretion, which can result in the excretion of very hyperosmolar urine. This latter effect is unique. The diuretic action is not affected by the acid-base status of the body.

b. Loss of K+: because thiazides increase the Na+ in the filtrate arriving at the distal tubule, more K+ is also exchanged, resulting in a continual loss of K+ from the body.

c. Loss of Mg2+: magnesium deficiency requiring supplementation can occur with chronic use of thiazide diuretics, particularly in elderly patients.

d. Decreased urinary calcium excretion: thiazide diuretics decrease the Ca2+ content of urine by promoting the reabsorption of Ca2+.

Thiazide diuretics — side effects & electrolyte changes
↓ Electrolytes in serum (common)
  • Na+ ↓ — hyponatremia
  • K+ ↓ — hypokalemia
  • Mg2+ ↓ — hypomagnesemia
↑ In serum
  • Uric acid (hyperuricemia)
  • Glucose (hyperglycemia)
  • Lipids (dyslipidemia)
Urine
  • More Na+ in the urine (DCT Na+/Cl− block)
  • ↓ Ca2+ in urine (↑ Ca2+ reabsorption → helps prevent stones)

Handout reminder: “Thiazides lose salt, but keep calcium!” — calcium stays home. Less Na+ today → lower BP tomorrow.

3. Therapeutic uses

UseReason / note
HypertensionMain use (thiazide diuretic is Step 1 of the treatment strategy)
Idiopathic hypercalciuriaThey inhibit urinary Ca2+ excretion; particularly beneficial for patients with calcium oxalate stones in the urinary tract
Nephrogenic diabetes insipidusThiazides can substitute for antidiuretic hormone in its treatment

“Thiazide takes K, Na & Volume DOWN; but uric acid, Ca & Glucose UP.”

B. Thiazide-Like Analogs

These compounds lack the thiazide structure, but share their mechanism of action.

DrugKey properties
1. ChlorthalidoneVery long duration of action → therefore often used to treat hypertension. Given once per day for this indication.
Product shown: Chlorthalidone Tablets USP 25 mg
2. MetolazoneMore potent than the thiazides and, unlike the thiazides, causes Na+ excretion in advanced renal failure.
3. Indapamide
  • Lipid-soluble, with a long duration of action; acts on the distal convoluted tubule.
  • At low doses, it shows significant antihypertensive action with minimal diuretic effects: ↑ urinary Na+ and water excretion → ↓ blood volume → ↓ blood pressure.
  • Metabolized and excreted by the gastrointestinal tract and the kidneys → therefore less likely to accumulate in patients with renal failure and may be useful in their treatment.
Renal failure — two different reasons

Metolazone still causes Na+ excretion in advanced renal failure (thiazides do not). Indapamide is less likely to accumulate in renal failure because it is eliminated by both the GI tract and the kidneys.

Quick Review

  • Thiazide-like diuretics contain the sulfonamide residue and share a similar mechanism.
  • Inhibit the Na+/Cl− co-transporter (cortical ascending loop of Henle + distal tubule); acid-base balance not usually affected.
  • Actions: very hyperosmolar urine (unique) · loss of K+ · loss of Mg2+ (chronic use, elderly) · ↓ urinary Ca2+.
  • Uses: hypertension, idiopathic hypercalciuria (calcium oxalate stones), nephrogenic diabetes insipidus.
  • Adverse: K+ depletion, hyponatremia, hyperuricemia, volume depletion, hypercalcemia, hyperglycemia.
  • Chlorthalidone: very long action, once daily · Metolazone: more potent, works in advanced renal failure · Indapamide: lipid-soluble, GI + renal elimination.

Loop (High-Ceiling) Diuretics

Bumetanide, furosemide, torsemide and ethacrynic acid are four diuretics that have their major action on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle. These drugs have the highest efficacy in mobilizing Na+ and Cl− from the body.

DrugNote from the handout

Ethacrynic acidShows greater side effects → its use is therefore limited
BumetanideMuch more potent than furosemide; its use is increasing
TorsemideMember of the class (acts on the ascending limb)
Product shown: Furosemide (Lasix®) 20 mg/2 mL solution for injection (IV/IM).

1. Mechanism of action

Loop diuretics inhibit the cotransport of Na+/K+/2Cl− in the luminal membrane in the ascending limb of the loop of Henle.

Why they are the most efficacious diuretics
  • The ascending limb accounts for the reabsorption of 25 to 30 percent of filtered NaCl.
  • Downstream sites are not able to compensate for this increased Na+ load.
  • They produce big amounts of urine.

2. Actions

The loop diuretics act among patients who have poor renal function or have not responded to thiazides.

Calcium: loop vs thiazide

Loop diuretics increase the Ca2+ content of urine, whereas thiazide diuretics decrease the Ca2+ concentration of the urine.

Loop → Calcium LOST
Thiazide → Calcium SAVED
  • In patients with normal serum Ca2+ concentrations, hypocalcemia does not result, because some Ca2+ is reabsorbed in the distal convoluted tubule.
  • However, hypomagnesemia can occur due to loss of Mg2+.
  • Loop diuretics increase prostaglandin synthesis. The prostaglandins have a role in their diuretic action, and NSAIDs such as indomethacin, which interfere in prostaglandin synthesis, can reduce the diuretic action of these agents.

  • Bumetanide, furosemide, torsemide, ethacrynic acid act on the ascending limb of the loop of Henle.
  • Inhibit Na+/K+/2Cl− cotransport; ascending limb reabsorbs 25–30% of filtered NaCl → most efficacious diuretics.
  • Work in poor renal function; ↑ urinary Ca2+ (Loop → calcium LOST); hypomagnesemia possible; NSAIDs reduce their action.
  • Uses: acute pulmonary edema of heart failure (drug of choice), hypercalcemia (with hydration), hyperkalemia.
  • Adverse: ototoxicity (esp. with aminoglycosides), hyperuricemia, acute hypovolemia, K+ depletion, hypomagnesemia.

Part B · Diuretics · Unit 07

Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

Potassium-sparing diuretics act in the collecting tubule to inhibit Na+ reabsorption and K+ excretion.

Caution

These drugs should be avoided in patients with renal dysfunction because of the increased risk of hyperkalemia.

A. Aldosterone Antagonists: Spironolactone and Eplerenone

1. Mechanism of action

  1. Spironolactone is a synthetic steroid that antagonizes aldosterone at intracellular cytoplasmic receptor sites.
  2. The spironolactone-receptor complex is inactive and prevents mediator protein synthesis.
  3. These mediator proteins normally stimulate the Na+/K+-exchange sites of the collecting tubule.
  4. Thus, a lack of mediator proteins prevents Na+ reabsorption and, therefore, K+ and H+ secretion.

2. Actions

  • In most states, blood levels of aldosterone are high, which is instrumental in retaining Na+.
  • When spironolactone is given to a patient with elevated circulating levels of aldosterone, the drug antagonizes the activity of the hormone → retention of K+ and excretion of Na+.
  • In patients who have no significant circulating levels of aldosterone, such as those with Addison disease (primary adrenal insufficiency), no diuretic effect of the drug occurs.
  • Eplerenone is a new aldosterone-receptor antagonist, with actions comparable to those of spironolactone. It is more selective for aldosterone receptors and may have less endocrine effects than spironolactone.

3. Therapeutic uses

UseDetail
a. DiureticUseful property of causing the retention of K+ (“K+ saver”). Because of this, spironolactone is often given in conjunction with a thiazide or loop diuretic to prevent the K+ excretion that would otherwise occur with these drugs.
b. Secondary hyperaldosteronismParticularly effective in clinical edema situations associated with secondary hyperaldosteronism. Spironolactone is the diuretic of choice in patients with hepatic cirrhosis with fluid in the peritoneal cavity (cirrhosis + ascites).
c. Heart failurePrevents the remodeling mediated by aldosterone; its use has been shown to decrease mortality associated with heart failure.
d. Polycystic ovary syndromeBlocks androgen receptors and inhibits steroid synthesis at high doses, thereby helping to balance increased androgen levels seen in this disorder (↓ androgen effects).

The handout’s “Health Benefits of Spironolactone” figure lists conditions it can help manage: high blood pressure, fluid buildup from liver disease, low potassium, PCOS, excessive hair growth or hair loss, symptoms of testosterone overproduction, and acne.

Adverse effects

EffectDetail
a. HyperkalemiaDose-dependent; increases with renal dysfunction or use of other potassium-sparing agents such as angiotensin-converting enzyme inhibitors and potassium supplements.
Gastrointestinal effectsSpironolactone frequently causes gastric upsets (nausea and abdominal upset) and can cause peptic ulcers.
b. Endocrine effectsBecause it chemically resembles some of the sex steroids, spironolactone may act at receptors in other organs to induce gynecomastia in male patients and menstrual irregularities in female patients.
Products shown: Spironolactone Tablets 50 mg; Aldactone® 25 mg (spironolactone).

B. Triamterene and Amiloride

  • Triamterene and amiloride block Na+ transport channels (shown in the handout figure as the luminal Na+ channel of the collecting tubule cell), resulting in a decrease in Na+/K+ exchange.
  • Like spironolactone, they prevent the loss of K+ that occurs with thiazides and furosemide.

Side effects of triamterene

  • Leg cramps (fluid/electrolyte changes)
  • Possibility of increased blood urea nitrogen (BUN):
    Triamterene→Diuresis→↓ Volume→↓ Renal perfusion→↑ BUN
  • Increased uric acid (gout)
  • K+ retention
Two different targets in the same cell

Spironolactone / eplerenone block the mineralocorticoid receptor (MR) that aldosterone binds. Amiloride / triamterene block the Na+ channel directly.

Quick Review

  • Act in the collecting tubule; avoid in renal dysfunction (hyperkalemia).
  • Spironolactone (synthetic steroid) blocks the cytoplasmic receptor → no mediator proteins → ↓ Na+ reabsorption and ↓ K+/H+ secretion.
  • No diuretic effect without circulating aldosterone (Addison disease). Eplerenone: more selective, fewer endocrine effects.
  • Uses: with thiazide/loop to prevent K+ loss · secondary hyperaldosteronism (diuretic of choice in cirrhosis with ascites) · heart failure (↓ mortality) · PCOS.
  • Adverse: hyperkalemia, gastric upset/peptic ulcers, gynecomastia and menstrual irregularities.
  • Triamterene and amiloride block Na+ channels; triamterene: leg cramps, ↑ BUN, uric acid (gout), K+ retention.

Part B · Diuretics · Unit 08

Carbonic Anhydrase Inhibitor

Acetazolamide inhibits the enzyme carbonic anhydrase in the proximal tubular epithelial cells.

1. Mechanism of action — acetazolamide

  • Inhibits carbonic anhydrase located intracellularly (cytoplasm) and on the apical membrane of the proximal tubular epithelium.
  • Inhibits the ability of the kidneys to reabsorb bicarbonate, so HCO3− is retained in the lumen and excreted with water, with marked elevation in urinary pH (basic).
  • The loss of HCO3− causes a hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and decreased diuretic efficacy following several days of therapy.

  • ↓ Bicarbonate reabsorption → HCO3− excreted → marked elevation in urinary pH (basic).
  • Causes hyperchloremic metabolic acidosis and decreased diuretic efficacy after several days.

Part B · Diuretics · Unit 09

Osmotic Diuretics

  • A number of simple, hydrophilic chemical substances that are filtered through the glomerulus, such as mannitol and urea, result in some degree of diuresis.
  • This is due to their ability to carry water with them into the tubular fluid.
  • If the filtered substance subsequently undergoes little or no reabsorption, it will cause an increase in urinary output by increasing the osmotic pressure.
Osmotic diuresis (handout example: glucose)

Limitation

Because osmotic diuretics are used to effect increased water excretion rather than Na+ excretion, they are not useful for treating conditions in which Na+ retention occurs.

Therapeutic uses

Osmotic diuretics are a mainstay of treatment for patients with:

  • Increased intracranial pressure, or
  • Acute renal failure due to shock, drug toxicities, and trauma.

Quick Review

  • Mannitol and urea: filtered, little or no reabsorption → ↑ osmotic pressure → ↑ urinary output.
  • Increase water excretion rather than Na+ excretion → not useful when Na+ retention occurs.
  • Mainstay for increased intracranial pressure and acute renal failure due to shock, drug toxicities and trauma.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 11

β-Adrenoceptor-Blocking Agents

β-Blockers are currently recommended as first-line drug therapy for hypertension when concomitant disease is present.

A. Actions

β-blockers reduce blood pressure:

  1. Primarily by decreasing cardiac output.
  2. They may also decrease sympathetic outflow from the central nervous system (CNS),
  3. and inhibit the release of renin from the kidneys,
  4. thus decreasing the formation of angiotensin II and the secretion of aldosterone.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 12

ACE Inhibitors (-pril)

The ACE inhibitors, such as enalapril and lisinopril, are recommended when:

  • the preferred first-line agents (diuretics or β-blockers) are contraindicated or ineffective, or
  • there are compelling reasons to use them, such as in diabetes mellitus.

A. Actions

Core action

ACE inhibitors lower blood pressure by reducing peripheral vascular resistance without reflexively increasing cardiac output, rate, or contractility.

  • They block the ACE that cleaves angiotensin I to form the potent vasoconstrictor angiotensin II.
  • The converting enzyme is also responsible for the breakdown of bradykinin; bradykinin increases the production of nitric oxide and prostacyclin by the blood vessels. Both nitric oxide and prostacyclin are potent vasodilators.
  • ACE inhibitors therefore decrease angiotensin II and increase bradykinin levels.
  • Vasodilation of both arterioles and veins occurs as a result of the combined effects of reduced vasoconstriction (diminished angiotensin II) and the potent vasodilating effect of increased bradykinin.
  • By reducing circulating angiotensin II, ACE inhibitors also decrease the secretion of aldosterone → decreased sodium and water retention.
  • ACE inhibitors reduce both cardiac preload and afterload, thereby decreasing cardiac work.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 13

Angiotensin II-Receptor Blockers (ARBs)

  • The ARBs are alternatives to the ACE inhibitors.
  • They block the AT1 receptors, decreasing the activation of AT1 receptors by angiotensin II.
  • Losartan is the prototypic ARB.
  • Pharmacologic effects similar to ACE inhibitors: produce arteriolar and venous dilation and block aldosterone secretion → lower blood pressure and decrease salt and water retention.
  • ARBs do not increase bradykinin levels.
  • ARBs decrease the nephrotoxicity of diabetes, making them an attractive therapy in hypertensive diabetics.
  • Adverse effects similar to ACE inhibitors, although the risks of cough and angioedema are significantly decreased.
  • ARBs are also fetotoxic and should not be used by women who are pregnant.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 14

Renin Inhibitor — Aliskiren

  • Aliskiren, a selective renin inhibitor, directly inhibits renin and thus acts earlier in the renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system than do ACE inhibitors or ARBs.
  • It can be combined with other antihypertensive drugs, such as diuretics, ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and calcium-channel blockers.
  • Available in a fixed-dose combination with valsartan as well as with hydrochlorothiazide.
  • Metabolized by CYP 3A4 and is, thus, subject to drug interactions.

Adverse effects and cautions

  • Diarrhea, especially at higher doses.
  • Cough and angioedema, but probably less often than ACE inhibitors.
  • Hyperkalemia is significantly more common in patients who received both valsartan and aliskiren.
  • As with ACE inhibitors and ARBs, aliskiren is contraindicated during pregnancy.

Calcium-Channel Blockers

  • Recommended when the preferred first-line agents are contraindicated or ineffective.
  • Effective in treating hypertension in patients with angina or diabetes.
Avoid

High doses of short-acting calcium-channel blockers should be avoided because of increased risk of myocardial infarction due to excessive vasodilation and marked reflex cardiac stimulation.

B. Actions

  • The intracellular concentration of calcium plays an important role in maintaining the tone of smooth muscle and in the contraction of the myocardium.
  • Calcium enters muscle cells through special voltage-sensitive calcium channels.
  • Calcium-channel antagonists block the inward movement of calcium by binding to L-type calcium channels in the heart and in smooth muscle of the coronary and peripheral arteriolar vasculature.
  • This causes vascular smooth muscle to relax, dilating mainly arterioles. Calcium channel blockers do not dilate veins.
Calcium channel blockers — mechanism of action
  1. Calcium enters muscle cells through L-type Ca2+ channels → smooth muscle tone; myocardial contraction.
  2. Calcium channel blockers bind to L-type Ca2+ channels and block calcium entry.
  3. Result: ↓ intracellular Ca2+
    Vascular smooth muscle: ↓ Ca2+ → relaxation (vasoconstricted arteriole → vasodilation)
    Cardiac muscle: ↓ Ca2+ → ↓ contraction

Cardiac myocyte figure: depolarization → Ca2+ enters via the L-type voltage-gated Ca channel → acts on RyR of the sarcoplasmic reticulum (SR) → release from the Ca2+ store → muscle contraction; calcium channel blockers act at the L-type channel. Calcium channel blockers do not dilate veins.

C. Therapeutic Uses

  • Useful in hypertensive patients who also have asthma, diabetes, angina, and/or peripheral vascular disease; unlike β-blockers, they do not have the potential to adversely affect these conditions.
  • All CCBs are useful in the treatment of angina.
  • In addition, diltiazem and verapamil are used in the treatment of atrial fibrillation.

Quick Review

  • Used when first-line agents are contraindicated or ineffective; effective with angina or diabetes.
  • Avoid high doses of short-acting CCBs (myocardial infarction risk).
  • Verapamil least selective · diltiazem less negative inotropic · dihydropyridines vascular-selective, little interaction with digoxin/warfarin.
  • Block L-type calcium channels; dilate mainly arterioles, not veins; diltiazem and verapamil used in atrial fibrillation.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 16

α-Adrenoceptor-Blocking Agents

  • Prazosin, doxazosin, and terazosin produce a competitive block of α1 adrenoceptors.
  • They decrease peripheral vascular resistance and lower arterial blood pressure by causing relaxation of both arterial and venous smooth muscle.
  • They cause only minimal changes in cardiac output, renal blood flow, and glomerular filtration rate.
  • Therefore, long-term tachycardia does not occur, but salt and water retention does.
  • Because of the side-effect profile and development of tolerance, α-blockers are rarely used as monotherapy in the treatment of hypertension.
  • Tamsulosin, an α1-blocker with greater selectivity for prostate muscle, has been used in the treatment of benign prostatic hyperplasia.
Product shown: Tamsulosin Sandoz® eco 0.4 mg (for functional symptoms of benign prostatic hyperplasia).

Quick Review

  • Prazosin, doxazosin, terazosin: competitive α1 block; relax arterial and venous smooth muscle.
  • Minimal change in CO, renal blood flow, GFR → no long-term tachycardia, but salt and water retention.
  • Rarely monotherapy; tamsulosin used in benign prostatic hyperplasia.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 19

Centrally Acting Adrenergic Drugs

A. Clonidine

  • This α2-agonist diminishes the central adrenergic outflow, decreasing the firing rate of the sympathetic nerves and the amount of norepinephrine release.
  • Clonidine does not decrease renal blood flow or glomerular filtration → useful in the treatment of hypertension complicated by renal disease.
  • Because it may cause sodium and water retention, clonidine may be administered in combination with a diuretic.
Withdrawal

Rebound hypertension occurs following abrupt withdrawal of clonidine. The drug should, therefore, be withdrawn slowly if the clinician wishes to change agents.

Handout figure (alpha adrenergic synapse): norepinephrine released from the synaptic vesicle acts on the postsynaptic α1 receptor; presynaptic α2 receptors mediate negative feedback on release; clonidine and dexmedetomidine act at the α2 receptor.

B. α-Methyldopa

  • This α2-agonist is converted to methyl-norepinephrine centrally to diminish adrenergic outflow from the CNS.
  • This leads to reduced total peripheral resistance and decreased blood pressure.
  • Cardiac output is not decreased, and blood flow to vital organs is not diminished.
  • Because blood flow to the kidney is not diminished, α-methyldopa is especially valuable in hypertensive patients with renal insufficiency.
  • It has been used in hypertensive pregnant patients.

Part C · Antihypertensive Drug Classes · Unit 20

Vasodilators

  • The direct-acting smooth muscle relaxants, such as hydralazine and minoxidil, have traditionally not been used as primary drugs to treat hypertension.
  • They act by producing relaxation of vascular smooth muscle, which decreases resistance and, therefore, blood pressure.
  • These agents produce reflex stimulation of the heart, resulting in the competing reflexes of increased myocardial contractility, heart rate, and oxygen consumption.

A. Hydralazine

  • Causes direct vasodilation, acting primarily on arteries and arterioles.
  • Result: decreased peripheral resistance, which in turn prompts a reflex elevation in heart rate and cardiac output.
  • Almost always administered in combination with:
    • a β-blocker, such as propranolol, metoprolol, or atenolol (to balance the reflex tachycardia), and
    • a diuretic (to decrease sodium retention).
  • Hydralazine monotherapy is an accepted method of controlling blood pressure in pregnancy.
Product shown: Hydralazine hydrochloride 25 mg tablets.

B. Minoxidil

  • Causes dilation of resistance vessels (arterioles) but not venules.
  • Administered orally for treatment of severe to malignant hypertension that is refractory to other drugs.
  • Reflex tachycardia and fluid retention may be severe and require the concomitant use of a loop diuretic and a β-blocker.
  • Causes serious sodium and water retention, leading to volume overload, edema, and congestive heart failure.
  • Acts by opening adenosine triphosphate-sensitive potassium channels in vascular smooth muscle cells.

Part D · Special Situations · Unit 21

Hypertensive Emergency

Definition

Hypertensive emergency is a rare but life-threatening situation in which the diastolic blood pressure is either greater than 150 mm Hg (with systolic blood pressure greater than 210 mm Hg).

Therapeutic goal: to rapidly reduce blood pressure.

Part D · Special Situations · Unit 22

Hypertension in Pregnancy

  • Mild hypertension associated with preeclampsia typically does not warrant treatment.
  • Severe hypertension (systolic pressures >150 mm Hg and diastolic blood pressures >100 mm Hg) is treated acutely with labetalol to prevent maternal cerebrovascular complications.
  • Avoid ACE inhibitors, ARBs, and aliskiren because these drugs may cause fetal injury or death.
  • Nitroprusside is contraindicated in the later stages of pregnancy due to possible fetal cyanide poisoning if used for more than 4 hours.
